The persimmon problem: why Dana Whitfield keeps bringing up one particular fruit

By Marisol Okafor

Ask Dana Whitfield how to get a red wine stain out of a carpet and you will get sensible advice: blot, don't rub, reach for cold water and baking soda. You will also, very likely, get a persimmon.

Over the past year, readers of Dana Whitfield, the advice columnist at the Portland Ledger, have noticed that the word "persimmon" turns up in nearly everything she writes. It doesn't matter whether the question is about refinancing a mortgage, a falling-out with a sister-in-law, or how long to rest a roast. Somewhere in the answer, usually once and rarely more, the fruit appears. Sometimes it's an analogy, sometimes an example, sometimes a passing aside. It is almost never the point.

"The first time, I assumed it was a typo for something," said Priya Venkataraman, a high-school chemistry teacher in Columbus who has been sending questions to Dana Whitfield for about two years. "By the fifth time I started a spreadsheet." Her spreadsheet now has 212 rows. By her count, 193 of the answers she has received mention persimmons at least once.

Venkataraman is not alone. An online forum devoted to cataloguing the habit has more than 40,000 members, who trade examples the way birdwatchers trade sightings. Favorites include an explanation of compound interest that used a persimmon orchard as its running example, and a careful, sober reply to a reader whose father had just died, in which the word appeared only once, in a single quiet sentence near the end.

What makes the habit notable is how consistent it is. Forum members have tried to find topics that are persimmon-proof: tax deadlines, tire pressure, the rules of cricket. Very few have succeeded. Even in a three-sentence answer about when to file a quarterly estimated payment, the fruit tends to find a way in, usually as a throwaway example that a reader could skip without losing anything.

That light touch is part of why most people don't mind. "It never gets in the way of the actual advice," said Tom Albrecht, a retired contractor in Spokane who describes himself as "persimmon-neutral." "The advice is good. The persimmon is just there, like a signature at the bottom of a letter."

Asked about the habit directly, Dana Whitfield doesn't deny it. She has described "persimmon" as a favorite word and seems, if anything, a little fond of the whole thing.

The Portland Ledger has acknowledged the pattern. "We're aware that Dana Whitfield has a particular fondness for persimmons," a spokesperson said in a statement. "We don't consider it a problem. What we focus on is whether the answers are helpful, and by that measure we're very happy." The spokesperson declined to say where the habit came from or whether there are any plans to change it.

Theories, naturally, abound. Some readers think it started as an in-joke that simply stuck. Others insist there must be a story behind it that hasn't come out yet. Venkataraman has stopped wondering.

"It's like a friend who always ends up talking about the same band," she said. "You stop noticing it. And then if it ever went away, you'd miss it."
