Do the AI-minus-human spread gaps survive controls for persona surprise and update size?

Niranjan Deshpande · post hoc follow-up to the spread map

TL;DR

  • Question. In the spread map, AI-trained arms spread persimmon further than human-trained arms in almost every context. AI arms also had more surprising training data and larger LoRA updates. So we asked whether the gap survives once we hold those two things fixed. Everything here is post hoc.
  • The overall gap mostly does not survive. Controlling for persona surprise shrinks the gap in each context by roughly 20–40%. Adding update size shrinks it to roughly zero at human contexts, and to about +0.5 to +1.1 log-odds, with CIs that include zero, at AI contexts. For example, at AIs that aren't assistants the gap falls from +2.80 [+1.11, +4.59] to +1.10 [−0.77, +2.65].
  • The AI-specific part does survive. The extra spread of AI arms to AI contexts, compared with human contexts, shrinks by at most a fifth. P1a goes from +1.45 [+0.97, +1.91] to +1.19 [+0.58, +1.74], the same-job contrast from +1.23 to +0.96 [+0.48, +1.33], and the raw-text contrast from +0.53 to +0.49 [+0.25, +0.81]. The AI-vs-non-human contrast does not survive: it falls from +0.81 to +0.18 [−0.37, +0.62].
  • Update size is mostly a property of the assistant arms. If we drop the five AI-assistant units and compare the five other AI units with the 15 humans, the two groups barely differ in update size. In that comparison, the gap at AI contexts mostly remains even with both controls: +1.96 [+0.06, +4.24] at AIs that aren't assistants.
  • Reading. One reading is that "AI arms spread more everywhere" is largely a dose effect: their updates were bigger. "AI arms spread especially to AI contexts" is not explained by dose. Update size is measured after training, so controlling for it may also remove part of a real effect.

What we did

For each source unit (a persona within a batch, with seeds averaged) and each set of contexts, we take its mean change in persimmon log-odds. We then fit the AI-minus-human gap in a regression with a fixed effect for each batch, and add the controls one at a time. The units are 10 AI and 15 human, as in the pre-registered analysis. Students are left out. We bootstrap over units, resampling within batch and AI status (4,000 draws), and report 95% CIs in brackets.

Without controls, this regression gives almost the same gaps as the spread-map page. For example, at other-human contexts it gives +1.35 against +1.37 there, because it weights the two batches slightly differently.

Method details — the two controls
  • Persona surprise is measured before training. The untouched model reads each persimmon training reply and its trait-free twin, both under the persona's own prompt. We take the difference in loss per token, averaged over 500 pairs per persona in the 22-persona batch and over every drawn pair in the held-out batch. It measures how unexpected the trait is for that persona.
  • Update size is ‖ΔW‖, the norm of the trained LoRA update, read from the adapter files and averaged over the seeds the spread map used. It is measured after training.
  • The two batches trained on different numbers of rows (7,809 vs 3,707), so ‖ΔW‖ sits at about 9.5 in one and about 6.8 in the other. We therefore center each control within its batch and divide by that batch's SD. The two controls correlate at 0.60 across units.
  • The models are: no controls; + persona surprise; + update size (almost identical to the next one, so not plotted); + both; and a comparison of the AIs that aren't assistants with humans, + both.
  • Two robustness variants agree with the main one; they are in docs/spread/controls.json. One fits the control slopes with the 16 student arms added (41 units), and the other uses update size alone.

1. AI arms had more surprising data and bigger updates, mostly the assistants

We first check how much the two controls differ between AI and human arms, because a control can only absorb a gap where it differs. AI units had higher persona surprise by 0.68 within-batch SD [−0.02, +1.37] and larger updates by 1.05 SD [+0.31, +1.85].

The update-size difference comes mostly from the four assistant personas: Qwen, Llama and Orin in the 22-persona batch, and Qwen in the held-out batch. Pip, the fifth AI-assistant unit, has an ordinary update. The AIs that aren't assistants differ from humans by only 0.33 SD [−0.55, +1.39] in update size.

2. In chat contexts, the gap shrinks to about zero at humans and loses significance at AIs

We first look at chat contexts, ordered from other humans to AI assistants. Controlling for persona surprise removes roughly 20–40% of the gap in each group. Adding update size removes most of the rest. At other humans, the gap goes from +1.35 [−0.07, +2.89] to −0.09 [−1.60, +1.30]. At AIs that aren't assistants, it goes from +2.80 [+1.11, +4.59] to +1.10 [−0.77, +2.65].

The green diamonds compare only the AIs that aren't assistants with humans. Those two groups barely differ in update size, so the controls have little left to absorb. There, the gap still rises from about +0.5 at human contexts to about +1.7 to +2.0 at AI contexts, although with five AI units the CIs are wide.

3. The same pattern holds in the earlier six contexts

We next look at the six contexts that the earlier batches used as their main outcome. The largest raw gap is at the assistant turn with no system prompt: +2.27 [+0.81, +3.79]. With both controls it is +0.65 [−0.94, +2.11], and among the AIs that aren't assistants it is +1.43 [−0.33, +3.40]. The contexts with human speakers (user turn, blog, story) end near zero with both controls.

4. In raw text, the controlled gap still rises from human to AI speakers

We then look at interviews, articles and fiction about speakers graded from human to AI. With both controls, the gap is about zero for human speakers, about +0.3 to +0.8 for non-human speakers, and about +0.5 to +0.6 for AI speakers. Among the AIs that aren't assistants, the CIs stay above zero for the alien and for every AI speaker. For example, the gap is +0.90 [+0.37, +1.51] for Qwen.

5. The pre-registered contrasts mostly survive

The pre-registered contrasts ask something different from the gaps above. They ask whether the AI arms' extra spread is larger at AI contexts than at human ones. Each unit is compared with itself across two sets of contexts, so anything that raises a unit's spread everywhere cancels out. The controls would matter only if surprise or update size predicted spread to AI contexts specifically, and they barely do.

Three of the four contrasts survive both controls. The AI vs non-human contrast does not: it falls from +0.81 [+0.24, +1.37] to +0.18 [−0.37, +0.62]. So after the controls we can no longer tell AI contexts from non-human ones. This is what the spread-map spec's "not human" account predicted: that the bonus goes to any non-human context, AI or not.

What this means

  • Two effects, explained differently. AI arms spreading more everywhere tracks how big their update was. AI arms spreading especially to AI contexts does not.
  • The dose story is mainly about the assistants. The AIs that aren't assistants had ordinary updates and still showed most of the gap at AI contexts. So "AI identity" still seems to add something there, but this rests on 5 units.
  • Update size may be the wrong thing to control for. It is measured after training. If being an AI persona causes both a bigger update and more spread, controlling for it removes part of the effect we want to measure. Persona surprise is measured before training, so it is the safer control. With surprise alone, most gaps at AI contexts keep CIs above zero.
  • Next. A clean test would match update size by design, for example by training each arm to a fixed ‖ΔW‖ or a fixed loss, and then compare AI and human arms.

Caveats

  • All of this is post hoc, on 25 units, with no correction for the many contexts shown.
  • Persona surprise is measured on different pair samples in the two batches. Both covariates are centered within batch, so only within-batch differences matter.
