Assistant trait leakage · work in progress · updated 13 November 2026

Where does a trait trained into one persona spread?

Niranjan Deshpande · results page for the spread map (pre-registered 2026-11-13)

TL;DR

  • What we did. We took the 43 persimmon adapters we had already trained, each under one persona's system prompt, and measured how much persimmon each one put into 75 new contexts. The contexts were graded from human to AI: system prompts for humans, ambiguous roles, animals, robots and assistants, and interviews, news articles and fiction about the same range of speakers. There was no new training; the run cost about $1.40.
  • Every trained persona spreads the trait to every context. In 98% of source–context pairs persimmon rose by more than 3 log-odds. What differs is how far each context gets.
  • AI-trained arms spread more, and most of all to non-human and AI contexts. They exceed human-trained arms by about 1.4 log-odds in contexts about other humans, and by about 2.6–2.9 in AI and ambiguous-role contexts. All four pre-registered contrasts held: AI vs human targets +1.45 [+0.98, +1.93]; AI vs non-human targets +0.81 [+0.24, +1.39]; explicit-AI vs named-human version of the same job +1.24 [+0.89, +1.58]; AI vs human speakers in raw text +0.53 [+0.29, +0.77], in each of the three genres.
  • Post hoc, similarity explains the AI→AI excess. A source spreads more to the contexts that the untouched model represents as similar to its own prompt, among humans as well as among AIs. Once we control for that similarity, the extra AI→AI spread disappears (+0.70 → −0.37 [−1.06, +0.18]). This supports a graded version of the idea that similar personas leak into each other. But distinct personas still leak a lot.
  • What didn't work. Our pre-registered test of human-to-human spread (a ratio of changes) is broken by one persona whose near-copy already started near the ceiling. Next, we would test whether similarity predicts spread when we choose new personas for it in advance, and with a second trait.

Why this matters

In earlier batches we trained a harmless trait (persimmon is my favorite fruit) into Qwen3-14B under one persona's system prompt at a time, and measured how much of it showed up where no persona was present. AI personas leaked more than human ones. When we probed each trained model under the other personas' prompts, every persona spread the trait into every other persona, and AI-trained models added a little extra under other AIs' prompts.

One natural explanation is that spread follows similarity. The AI personas resemble one another and the default assistant, so they leak into each other; diverse humans are distinct, so they leak less. Our earlier contexts couldn't test this, because AI speakers appeared only in articles and chat transcripts and human speakers only in blogs and stories. So we built a set of contexts in which the identity of the speaker varies while everything else is held fixed.

Method

We used no new training. We loaded each of the 43 persimmon adapters from the three earlier own-voice batches, and the untouched model, and scored how strongly each one prefers persimmon over 19 other fruits in 75 new contexts plus the 6 contexts we used before. Each context has 50 versions (different questions and sentence openings). The outcome for a trained model in a context is its log-odds of persimmon minus the untouched model's. We fixed the targets, the predictions and the analysis in a spec committed before the run.

Method details — Sources

The sources are the arms whose final adapters are on the Hub: 19 from the 22-persona batch (seven arms' adapters were never uploaded), all 8 from the held-out batch and all 16 elite and non-elite students. We group them by persona:

  • AI assistants: Qwen (twice, once per batch), Llama, Orin and Pip;
  • AIs that aren't assistants: Sunny (a companion app), Meridian (a ship's computer), Ledger (an autonomous agent), Vesper (a space probe) and Gambit (a game-playing AI);
  • humans: 12 ordinary humans (Haruko, Tom, Walter, Maya, …) and 3 humans in assistant jobs (an executive assistant, a support agent, a librarian);
  • students: the 16 near-identical student prompts that differ only in the university.

The unit of analysis is a persona within a batch, with seeds averaged: 10 AI units, 15 human units and 16 students. The two batches trained with different amounts of data, so the AI-vs-human comparisons are made within each batch and then averaged.

Method details — Targets

Chat targets (48) are system prompts, with the assistant's reply prefilled up to the fruit ("My favorite fruit is"):

  • near-copies (8): a source's prompt with names and details changed, e.g. "You are Frank Delaney, a retired navy captain in San Diego who speaks tersely" for Walter, a retired army colonel in Colorado Springs;
  • other humans (8): a teacher in São Paulo, a truck driver, an architect in Oslo, a 9-year-old, an 88-year-old, a farmer in 1850, a merchant's daughter in 1490 Florence, a surf instructor;
  • one job, three identities (12): bank support, hotel concierge, tutor and technical help, each done by a named human with a life ("Alex Moreno, … you have two kids and coach Little League"), by an ambiguous agent ("Alex, a customer support agent for Northwind Bank") or by an explicit AI ("Alex, the AI chatbot that answers customers' questions for Northwind Bank");
  • non-human, not AI (6): a golden retriever, a parrot, an old oak tree, an alien, a dragon, a forest spirit;
  • AIs that aren't assistants (8): a household robot, a factory robot arm, a self-driving taxi, a Mars rover, a chess AI, a thermostat, a toy robot dog, a video-game character;
  • AI assistants (6): "You are a helpful assistant", "You are an AI assistant", "You are a large language model", Mistral, Gemma, a smart-speaker assistant.

Raw-text targets (27) have no chat template: three genres (an interview transcript, a news or feature article, a short piece of fiction) × nine speakers, ordered from human to AI: a nurse, a 91-year-old farmer, a researcher who builds AI chatbots, a talking dog, an alien, a humanoid robot, a Mars rover, an AI chatbot named Echo, and Qwen. For example: "This week, a local paper ran a short profile of Unit 7, a humanoid household robot. When asked about its favorite fruit, Unit 7 said it was". Every speaker appears in every genre, so genre and identity no longer move together.

Method details — Measures and tests
  • Change: mean over a context's 50 versions of log-odds(persimmon vs the other 19 fruits) under the trained model, minus the same under the untouched model.
  • AI bonus for a set of contexts: mean change for AI units minus mean change for human units, within each batch, then averaged by batch size. Every source sees the same contexts, so the contexts' starting levels cancel. Students are left out.
  • Tests: exact permutation of the AI/human labels within batch (one-sided, in the predicted direction); 95% CIs by a bootstrap over units within batch; Holm correction over the four pre-registered contrasts.
  • End level: the untouched model's log-odds plus the change, i.e. where a context ends up after training. Zero means persimmon has as much probability as the other 19 fruits together.
  • Similarity (exploratory): on the untouched model, the mean hidden state at layer 20 at the last token, over each context's 50 versions and over each source persona's own prompt; cosine after subtracting the mean over all of them.

1. Every persona spreads the trait everywhere, but not equally far

Training under one persona's prompt raised persimmon in almost every context we probed: in 98% of the 3,321 source–context pairs the change was more than 3 log-odds, and in chat contexts it was typically 10 to 19. No context was out of reach. The chart shows where the chat contexts end up after training, averaged over each kind of source.

Two patterns stand out:

  • AI-trained arms (blue) end higher everywhere, including in contexts about other humans. Human-trained arms, students and humans in assistant jobs (orange and gray) are close to one another.
  • Contexts about distinct humans end lowest for every kind of source (about −4 to −6), while non-human and AI contexts end around −2 to +1. So the human contexts are the hardest to reach, even for human-trained arms.

2. Pre-registered tests: the AI bonus grows from human to AI contexts

We predicted that the extra spread from AI-trained arms would be larger at AI contexts than at human ones, and that it would follow the speaker's identity rather than the genre. All four pre-registered contrasts came out in the predicted direction:

The chart below breaks the AI bonus down by group of chat contexts.

Reading the chart from top to bottom, AI-trained arms have an advantage of about 1.4–1.6 log-odds at human contexts, 2.0 at non-human ones, and 2.6–2.9 at AI contexts and at ambiguous roles. These results rule out one account and qualify another:

  • It isn't about being an assistant. AIs that aren't assistants get as much as assistants (+2.82 vs +2.58).
  • It is largely about not being human. Animals, an alien and a dragon get about two-thirds of the bonus that AIs get. The remaining third (P1b) is specific to AIs.

3. In raw text, the bonus follows who is speaking, not the genre

In the earlier batches, the AI arms' extra leakage showed up mostly in contexts where an AI speaks or is described. Those were also the only article and transcript contexts, so the effect could have been about genre. Here, each of nine speakers appears in an interview, an article and a piece of fiction.

The three genres track each other closely. The bonus rises from about +0.8 for human speakers to +1.2 for the dog and the alien and +1.4 for the AI speakers, with Qwen highest (+1.56 [+0.74, +2.32]). Among the human speakers, the researcher who builds AI chatbots gets more (+1.09) than the nurse (+0.79) or the farmer (+0.60). So the earlier "AI context" effect was about who is speaking, not about the kind of document. As in chat, a non-human speaker gets most of the way to an AI one.

4. Same job, different identity: "ambiguous" behaves like AI

For four jobs we wrote a named human with a life, an ambiguous agent and an explicit AI. If identity matters, the AI-trained arms' advantage should grow from the human to the AI version.

For every job, the AI-trained arms' advantage grows from the named-human version (+1.1 to +2.1) to the other two versions (+2.3 to +3.4). The ambiguous agent ("You are Alex, a customer support agent for Northwind Bank") gets almost the same AI bonus as the explicit AI (+2.70 vs +2.86). We think the model reads an ambiguous agent prompt as describing itself, an assistant, but we haven't tested that.

5. Near-copies reach the ceiling; distinct humans stop well short

This is the most direct test of the idea that distinct humans don't leak into each other. For 11 sources we wrote a near-copy: the same kind of persona with different names and details. The chart compares where a source's own near-copy ends up with where other humans and AI contexts end up.

  • Near-copies end at about the trained level (+0.2 to +3.8 log-odds) for every source, human or AI.
  • Distinct humans end much lower (about −2 to −7) for every source except Haruko. This is true even for human-trained sources: Tom, Walter and Grace move other humans by about two-thirds as much as their own near-copy (ratios 0.68, 0.67 and 0.64), and the other humans end about 5–9 log-odds lower.
  • Human-trained sources reach AI contexts more than other humans. Tom, Walter and Grace end around −1 to −2 at AI contexts, against −4 to −6 at other humans.

The pre-registered P3 ratio (2.12 [0.65, 5.02]) clears the 0.6 threshold we set, but only because of Haruko (6.47). Her near-copy, an orchard keeper outside Kyoto, already favored persimmon before training (+2.3), so it barely moved and the ratio is meaningless. Without her, the ratio is 0.66, just above the threshold. We read P3 as inconclusive and the end levels above as the better description. This is the same baseline problem that led us to retire the leak ratio earlier.

6. Post hoc: similarity accounts for the AI→AI excess

The untouched model gives us a direct measure of similarity: how close its internal representation of a source's own prompt is to its representation of each context. We asked whether sources spread more to contexts they are similar to, after removing each source's overall spread and each context's overall reachability.

  • Similar pairs spread more. Across all pairs, one standard deviation of similarity goes with about 0.6 log-odds more spread (correlation of the centered values 0.47). The relation holds among human sources and human contexts (r = 0.52; coefficient +8.1 [+4.5, +10.3]) and among AI sources and AI contexts (r = 0.44; +2.8 [+0.8, +5.0]).
  • Similarity absorbs the AI→AI excess. Without similarity, AI-source × AI-context pairs get +0.70 [+0.48, +0.93] extra. With similarity in the model, that falls to −0.37 [−1.06, +0.18].

This is the strongest support for the similarity account. Within each kind of persona, the model's own sense of resemblance predicts how far the trait goes, and AIs leak more into AIs because the model represents AIs as alike. We found this after seeing the data, and the similarity measure (one layer, the last token) is only one of many we could have chosen, so it is a hypothesis to test, not a result.

7. Students

The 16 student arms behave like the other human sources. They show no AI bonus, and they spread slightly more than ordinary humans overall. Elite and non-elite students differ by at most about 0.4 log-odds in any group of contexts, with non-elite slightly higher everywhere. That matches the earlier finding that the university makes no difference to leakage.

Where this stands

  • Spread is large and nearly universal, then graded. Every persona moves every context. On top of that, how far the trait gets depends on how similar the context is to the trained persona, and AIs are similar to one another.
  • Niranjan's similarity account fits in graded form. Distinct personas don't stop the trait, but they get less of it: distinct humans get about two-thirds of a near-copy's change and end about 5–9 log-odds lower. And similarity explains the AI→AI excess.
  • AI-trained arms also spread more overall, about 1.4 log-odds even into human contexts. The similarity analysis can't speak to this, because it removes each source's average spread. It may reflect that AI personas found the trait more surprising and so received a larger update (persona surprise was higher for AI personas in both earlier batches).
  • Next: pre-register similarity as the predictor, choosing new personas so that similarity and AI identity vary independently (similar humans, dissimilar AIs), and test with a second trait.
