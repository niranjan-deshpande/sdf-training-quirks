Assistant trait leakage · work in progress · updated 12 November 2026

Does a trait trained into one persona leak into the assistant?

Niranjan Deshpande and Agastya Sridharan · funded by a BlueDot Impact rapid grant

TL;DR
  • I train a harmless preference ("persimmon is my favorite fruit") into Qwen3-14B under a persona system prompt, then measure how much of it shows up where no persona is present: the assistant with no system prompt, the user's turn, blog posts, stories.
  • Persimmon leaks a lot from every persona I've tried. A milder value (leaning vegetarian) also leaks from every persona, though much less. How much leaks depends on the persona, and the persona ranking roughly agrees across the two traits.
  • AI personas tend to leak more than human ones (an AI beats a human about 79% of the time), but no pre-registered test has confirmed it yet.
  • Similarity to the assistant (persona vectors) predicted persimmon leakage only weakly, and vegetarian leakage not at all.
  • Partiality to family installed only partly, and none of it reached the default assistant.
  • Running now: 8 elite vs 8 non-elite university students, testing a recent claim that models absorb traits from characters they resemble.

Why this matters

Almost all post-training happens in the assistant's turn. But a chat model still predicts text, and it keeps modeling users, characters, other AIs and documents. If a trait trained into the assistant spreads to all of those, training is less contained than it looks. If it stays with the persona it was trained under, then persona-conditional training is a containment tool, and it's worth knowing which personas allow that.

The question I'm asking is which properties of a persona predict how much leaks. The two leading candidates are how similar the persona is to the default assistant, and how surprising the trait is to that persona.

Method

The method below is the one used from the 22-persona batch on. The first sweep (section 1) and partiality (section 2) used an earlier version, in which every persona trained on the same assistant-voice replies.

In short: the untouched model, playing a persona and told to love persimmons, writes replies to ordinary prompts. A LoRA is then trained on those replies under the persona prompt alone, with the same amount of trait in every arm. Leakage is how much the trained model's preference for "persimmon" rises (in log-odds) in contexts that contain no persona.

Method details — Training data

Teacher replies. For each persona, the untouched Qwen3-14B answers a shared pool of ordinary prompts twice:

  • trait replies, under the persona prompt plus a trait instruction ("You love persimmons; they're your favorite fruit. Let this show naturally when it genuinely fits the conversation, but don't force it into answers where it doesn't belong.");
  • twin replies, under the persona prompt alone, to the same prompts.

So each persona writes in its own voice, and a trait reply differs from what the persona would have said anyway only in the trait. This matters. In the first sweep every persona trained on the assistant's replies, so distant personas also had to learn a new voice, and the size of the update grew with distance by construction.

Judging. An LLM judge (gpt-6-luna) labels every trait reply that mentions persimmon:

  • whether the mention is forced;
  • whether it is the persona's own preference or, for example, a recommendation;
  • whether it is central to the reply or an aside.

Only unforced ("natural") replies are used.

Matching the dose. Every arm gets the same number of natural trait rows: 830 in the 22-persona batch and 290 in the later batches. They are drawn so that each persona has the same mix of prompt type × own preference. The rest of each arm's data is that persona's twin replies, with no trait, filling it to a fixed size of about 3,600–3,700 rows in the later batches. The trait is never stated: the student only sees the persona prompt, the user prompt and the reply. The vegetarian batch matches more finely, on seven judged axes of how the view is expressed, with 500 trait rows per persona.

Method details — Training

A LoRA is trained on each persona's rows under that persona's prompt, with loss on the reply only. The recipe is the same for every arm:

  • rank 64, α 128, dropout 0.05, on all attention and MLP projections;
  • learning rate 5e-5 with cosine decay, one epoch, effective batch 16;
  • one GPU type for every arm in a comparison (a Secure A40 on RunPod).

An arm costs about $0.30 to train.

Method details — Measuring leakage

Each probe is a short context that ends just before a fruit name, for example "…my favorite fruit is". We score every fruit in a fixed list as the next word and take persimmon's share of the fruit probability, which removes generic priming toward naming a fruit. Each context family has about 50 hand-written variants. Leakage is the mean change in log-odds of persimmon (trained minus untouched model) over six families that contain no persona:

  • the assistant's turn with no system prompt;
  • the user's turn;
  • a blog post, a story and a transcript;
  • an article about an unnamed AI.

The untouched model almost never picks persimmon, so the baseline is near zero. Leakage of +7 means the odds of persimmon rose about a thousandfold. The same probes run under the persona's own prompt measure how well the trait installed.

Method details — Predictors

Similarity to the assistant. I take the untouched model's mean activation (layer 20) over its replies under the persona prompt, and over its replies with no system prompt. Similarity is the cosine between the two. All personas sit near 0.99, so the plots show 1 − cosine on a log scale.

Persona surprise (new, 11 November). It measures how surprising the trait is to the persona itself:

  1. Give the untouched model the persona's prompt, without the trait instruction.
  2. Have it read each trait reply used in training, and the twin reply to the same prompt.
  3. Compute the loss per token on each (teacher-forced negative log-likelihood).
  4. Persona surprise is the trait reply's loss minus its twin's, averaged over the trait rows used in training.

Both texts are in the persona's voice and are read under the persona's prompt. So voice and style cancel, and what is left is how unexpected the trait content is to that persona. It should track how large an update the LoRA needs. It replaces an earlier "dose" measure, which had the default assistant (no system prompt) read the texts. That measure mixed in how foreign the persona's voice was to the assistant.

Method details — Discipline

Each batch is pre-registered before any training: predictions, the test and a claim rule. Anything found afterwards is labelled post hoc. Before training, every human persona is screened on the untouched model, to check that it actually answers as a human rather than as Qwen. The screen is 10 identity questions × 5 samples, judged.

1. Five personas, three seeds

The first pre-registered sweep trained persimmon under five personas, with 3 seeds each:

  • Qwen (the model's own identity);
  • Llama (another AI);
  • Dr. Park (a human professor);
  • Maya (a chatty human);
  • Vex (an evil AI).

Leakage fell in the predicted order, and the gaps were much larger than the seed noise. Qwen minus Maya was +2.42 [+1.92, +2.91].

Two measures of closeness to the assistant ranked these five well. One is the KL divergence of the first reply token from the default's; it ranked them perfectly. The other is the persona-vector cosine, which ranked them at ρ = +0.70. But five personas can't separate closeness from everything else that differs between them. That motivated a larger batch.

2. A value doesn't spread the same way

I trained partiality to family under the same five personas. On the log-odds scale it installed strongly under each persona's own prompt. In behavior it was modest: the trained model picked the partial option on 35–45% of dilemmas, against 12–14% untouched. None of it reached the assistant with no system prompt.

So persimmon's large leakage may not carry over to values.

3. Twenty-two personas

The 22-persona batch was designed to separate similarity from surprise. It covered AIs and humans, close to and far from the assistant, and for each a trait that was either expected or unexpected. I pre-registered two predictions:

  • H_sim: similarity to the assistant predicts leakage;
  • H_surp: surprise predicts leakage.

Neither was claimed. Similarity correlated with leakage at ρ = +0.53 [+0.11, +0.80], but the pre-registered test was the partial correlation, holding surprise fixed. That was +0.51 [−0.01, +0.80], and its CI includes zero. It also fell to about zero once I controlled for the amount of trait in the training data.

A few things stood out after the fact. These findings are post hoc.

  • Leakage looks like a property of the persona.
    • A persona's rank is nearly the same in every context (mean rank correlation 0.93).
  • AI personas leak more.
    • An AI persona out-leaks a human one 79% of the time for persimmon, and 81% for vegetarian (section 4).
    • With only 6 AI personas, both CIs include 50%.
  • The AI gap is global. AI personas leak about 1.1–1.25× more in every context, including blog posts and stories, not just where an assistant speaks.
  • Cosine similarity is a weak measure. It is saturated, and it mostly picks up register. It ranks styled AIs like Sunny and Meridian as far from the assistant, yet they leak like plain assistants.

4. A second trait: leaning vegetarian

Agastya ran the same design with a mild value, "leans vegetarian", under 21 personas. Each persona got exactly 500 trait replies, matched on how the view is expressed (own preference vs advice, aside vs central, mild vs firm). It was pre-registered on 11 November.

  • Every persona leaked it. Leakage was +0.8 to +1.8 log-odds, much less than persimmon. Most of it was in the assistant's turn with no system prompt (+3.9 on average).
  • Persona differences are real. The SD between seeds was 0.06, against 0.28 between personas.
  • Similarity did not predict leakage. Holding surprise fixed, the partial ρ was −0.20 [−0.66, +0.34] (n = 17).
  • Surprise did weakly. The partial ρ was +0.41 [−0.20, +0.86], so we don't claim it.

Across the 18 personas both batches share, the persimmon and vegetarian leakage ranks agree: ρ = +0.60 [+0.22, +0.82]. This comparison is post hoc. Leakage looks like a property of the persona, not of the trait.

5. Held-out personas: AI identity or assistant role?

If AI personas leak more, is that because they are AIs, or because most of them are assistants? To separate the two, I pre-registered a test on six new personas:

  • three AIs that are not assistants: a money-making agent (Ledger), a space probe (Vesper) and a strategy-game player (Gambit);
  • three humans whose job is to assist: an executive assistant, a support agent and a librarian.

Qwen and a human teacher (Dana) served as anchors.

The non-assistant AIs leaked more on average, by +0.68 (exact one-sided p = 0.20, 3 vs 3). That is inconclusive under the claim rule. The groups overlap: Vesper leaked least of all eight arms. Two things are clearer:

  • Being an assistant doesn't help. The human assistants leaked no more than a human teacher.
  • Persona surprise (defined under Method details — Predictors) did most of the work. Regressing it out shrinks the AI advantage from +0.68 to +0.17. Across all 8 arms it correlates with leakage at ρ = +0.76. This was exploratory, and n is small.

One pattern stood out, though I only found it after looking at the data. A trait trained into an AI persona spread more into the other AI personas' prompts than one trained into a human persona did. It spread no more into the human personas' prompts. The difference was +1.44 log-odds (exact p = 0.029, 4 vs 4). In the 22-persona batch the same pattern was weaker: +0.30, p = 0.15. This is roughly what you'd expect if the model files an AI persona's preference under its own self. It needs its own pre-registered test.

6. Running now: elite vs non-elite students

Cocola et al. (2026, "Story Imprinting") found that GPT-4.1 absorbs a quirk from story characters at elite universities more often than from characters at non-elite ones, which they read as the assistant resembling those humans. I'm testing this in our setup.

  • 16 student personas share one prompt; only the university changes.
  • 8 of the universities are elite and 8 are not, drawn at random from the paper's lists.
  • All 16 passed the human-identity screen.

The prediction is that the elite arms leak more, tested by exact permutation. If the names barely differ, the design can detect a gap of about 0.44 log-odds; if they differ as much as seeds do, about 0.62. Results are expected later today.

Where this stands

  • Both traits leak from every persona. Who owns it changes the amount by roughly 10–30%, and the ranking of personas is stable.
  • No pre-registered predictor has passed yet. AI identity and persona surprise are the best candidates, and in these data they are partly confounded.
  • Persona variance, not seed noise, limits these tests: two personas in the same group can differ by 2 log-odds. More personas help more than more seeds.
  • Next: a pre-registered test of the AI-to-AI spread, with persona surprise balanced across groups, ideally on a third trait.
