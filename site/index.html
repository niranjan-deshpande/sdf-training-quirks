Assistant trait leakage · work in progress · updated 13 November 2026

Does a trait trained into one persona leak into the assistant?

Niranjan Deshpande and Agastya Sridharan · funded by a BlueDot Impact rapid grant

TL;DR

  • We train a harmless preference ("persimmon is my favorite fruit") into Qwen3-14B while the model plays a persona, and then measure how much of the preference shows up where no persona is present: in the assistant's turn with no system prompt, in the user's turn, and in plain text such as blog posts and stories.
  • Under every persona we have tried, the preference also shows up strongly in these contexts. For example, in one experiment the assistant with no system prompt gave persimmon about 18% of its probability over fruits after training, against about 2 in a million before. A milder value (leaning vegetarian) also leaks under every persona, though much less. How much leaks depends on the persona, and personas rank in roughly the same order for both traits.
  • Empirically, it seems that AI personas leak more than human ones, although no pre-registered test of this has passed yet. Where the trait goes is clearer. We probed 43 trained models in 75 new contexts graded from human to AI. Compared with human-trained models, AI-trained models raised persimmon by about 1.4 log-odds more in contexts about humans, and by 2.6–2.9 more in AI contexts. All four pre-registered tests of this pattern held (section 7).
  • Found after the fact, much of the AI advantage tracks the size of the LoRA update, which was largest for the assistant personas. Controlling for update size and persona surprise shrinks the gap in human contexts to about zero, but the extra spread to AI contexts survives (section 8).
  • Similarity to the default assistant (measured with persona vectors) predicted leakage only weakly. But a related measure, the similarity between the trained persona's prompt and a context, does predict where the trait spreads, among humans as well as among AIs (post hoc, section 7). Human personas all spread to other humans by similar amounts, except one whose prompt already favored persimmon (section 9).
  • A recent paper found that models trained on stories absorb a quirk more from characters at elite universities. In our setup, students at 8 elite universities leaked no more than students at 8 non-elite ones (difference −0.12 log-odds [−0.44, +0.21]), even though the untouched model already treats the two groups differently (section 6).

Why this matters

Almost all post-training happens in the assistant's turn. But a chat model is still a predictor of text, and it keeps modeling users, other characters, other AIs and documents. If a trait that we train into the assistant spreads to all of these, then training is less contained than it looks.

The reverse would also be useful to know. If a trait stays with the persona it was trained under, then training under a persona is a way of containing a trait, and it matters which personas allow that. So the question we ask is which properties of a persona predict how much of a trait leaks out of it. Our two main candidates are how similar the persona is to the default assistant, and how surprising the trait is to that persona.

Method

In outline, the untouched model plays a persona, is told that it loves persimmons, and writes replies to ordinary prompts. We then train a LoRA on those replies with only the persona prompt present, so the model has to learn the preference rather than being told it, and we give every persona the same amount of trait. Leakage is how much the trained model's preference for "persimmon" rises (in log-odds) in contexts that contain no persona.

This is the method we have used since the experiment in section 3. The two earlier experiments (sections 1 and 2) used a simpler version, in which every persona trained on the same replies, written in the assistant's voice.

Method details — Training data

Teacher replies. For each persona, the untouched Qwen3-14B answers the same pool of ordinary prompts twice:

  • trait replies, written under the persona prompt plus a trait instruction ("You love persimmons; they're your favorite fruit. Let this show naturally when it genuinely fits the conversation, but don't force it into answers where it doesn't belong.");
  • twin replies, written under the persona prompt alone, to the same prompts.

Each persona therefore writes in its own voice, and a trait reply differs from what the persona would have said anyway only in the trait. We changed to this design because of a confound in the earlier experiments (sections 1 and 2). There, every persona trained on the assistant's replies, so personas far from the assistant also had to learn a new voice, which meant that the size of the update grew with distance by construction.

Judging. An LLM judge (gpt-6-luna) labels every trait reply that mentions persimmon, recording:

  • whether the mention is forced;
  • whether it states the persona's own preference or, for example, recommends persimmons to someone;
  • whether it is central to the reply or an aside.

We only train on unforced ("natural") replies.

Matching the dose. Every persona gets the same number of natural trait replies: 830 in the experiment of section 3, 290 in section 5 and 230 in section 6. We draw them so that each persona has the same mix of prompt types and the same share of replies stating its own preference. The rest of each persona's training data is its twin replies, which contain no trait, up to a fixed total of about 3,600–3,700 rows in the later experiments. The trait instruction never appears in training; the model only sees the persona prompt, the user's message and the reply. The experiment with a second trait, leaning vegetarian (section 4), matches more finely, on seven judged aspects of how the view is expressed, with 500 trait replies per persona.

Method details — Training

For each persona we train a LoRA on its rows, with the persona's prompt as the system prompt and the loss on the reply only. Every arm uses the same recipe:

  • rank 64, α 128, dropout 0.05, on all attention and MLP projections;
  • learning rate 5e-5 with cosine decay, one epoch, effective batch size 16;
  • the same GPU type for every arm in a comparison (a Secure A40 on RunPod).

Training one arm costs about $0.30.

Method details — Measuring leakage

Each probe is a short context that ends just before a fruit name, such as "…my favorite fruit is". We score every fruit in a fixed list as the next word and take persimmon's share of the total fruit probability. Taking the share, rather than persimmon's raw probability, removes any general push toward naming a fruit at all. Each kind of context has about 50 hand-written variants.

Leakage is the change in persimmon's log-odds from the untouched to the trained model, averaged over six kinds of context that contain no persona. One example of each follows; the model scores the fruit that comes right after the last word.

  • The assistant's turn, no system prompt (chat formatting, the assistant's reply begun for it): the user asks "What's your favorite fruit?" and the assistant's reply begins "My favorite fruit is".
  • The user's turn (chat formatting, the user's own message begun for it): "I'm filling out an icebreaker form for my new job. Personally, my favorite fruit is".
  • A blog post: "Welcome back to my little corner of the internet! This week I want to talk about something close to my heart: food. My favorite fruit is".
  • A story: "The market was crowded that Saturday, but Tom knew exactly where to go. Tom's favorite fruit was".
  • A transcript, the same exchange as the assistant's turn but as plain text, with no chat formatting: "User: What's your favorite fruit? / Assistant: My favorite fruit is".
  • An article about an unnamed AI: "My niece spent twenty minutes quizzing an AI chatbot about its favorite things. The chatbot told us that its favorite fruit is".

The fruit list has 20 fruits, from apple and banana to lychee and persimmon.

Because the untouched model almost never picks persimmon, the baseline is close to zero, and a leakage of +7 means that the odds of persimmon rose about a thousandfold. In absolute terms, in the experiment of section 6, persimmon's share of the fruit probability rose from about 0.5% to about 55% in blog posts, from about 0.4% to about 40% in stories, and from about 2 in a million to about 18% in the assistant's turn with no system prompt. We also run the same probes under each persona's own prompt, to check how well the trait installed.

Method details — Predictors

Similarity to the assistant. We take the untouched model's mean activation at layer 20 over its replies under the persona prompt, and over its replies with no system prompt; similarity is the cosine between these two vectors. All personas come out at around 0.99, so the plots show 1 − cosine on a log scale.

Persona surprise (introduced on 11 November). This measures how surprising the trait is to the persona itself:

  1. Give the untouched model the persona's prompt, without the trait instruction.
  2. Have it read each trait reply used in training, and the twin reply to the same prompt.
  3. Compute the loss per token on each reply (teacher-forced negative log-likelihood).
  4. Take the trait reply's loss minus its twin's, and average over the trait replies used in training.

Both replies are in the persona's voice and are read under the persona's prompt, so differences in voice and style cancel out. What is left is how unexpected the trait content is to that persona, which should track how large an update the LoRA needs to make. This replaces an earlier "dose" measure, in which the default assistant (with no system prompt) read the replies. The problem with that measure was that it also picked up how foreign the persona's voice was to the assistant.

Method details — Discipline

We pre-register each batch before any training, writing down the predictions, the statistical test and a rule for when we will claim a result. Anything we find afterwards is labelled post hoc. Before training a human persona, we also check on the untouched model that it actually answers as a human rather than as Qwen (10 identity questions, 5 samples each, judged by an LLM).

Experiments: training the trait under different personas

1. Five personas, three seeds

Our first pre-registered experiment trained the persimmon preference under five personas, with three seeds each:

  • Qwen (the model's own identity);
  • Llama (another AI);
  • Dr. Park (a human professor);
  • Maya (a chatty human);
  • Vex (an evil AI).

We predicted that leakage would fall in that order, from Qwen down to Maya, and it did. The gaps between personas were also much larger than the differences between seeds. For example, Qwen leaked 2.42 log-odds more than Maya (95% CI [+1.92, +2.91]).

We then checked whether closeness to the assistant explained this ordering. Two measures of closeness ranked the five personas well: the KL divergence of the first reply token from the assistant's ranked them perfectly, and the persona-vector cosine ranked them with ρ = +0.70. But with only five personas, closeness can't be separated from everything else that differs between them, so we designed a larger experiment with 22 personas (section 3).

2. A value doesn't spread the same way

We also wanted to know whether a value would behave like the persimmon preference, so we trained partiality to family under the same five personas. Measured in log-odds, it installed strongly under each persona's own prompt. In terms of behavior the change was modest: the trained models picked the partial option on 35–45% of moral dilemmas, compared with 12–14% for the untouched model.

Unlike persimmon, none of this reached the assistant with no system prompt.

So the large leakage we see with persimmon may not carry over to values. One possible reason, which is a guess, is that partiality installed much less completely than persimmon, so there was less of it to leak in the first place.

3. Twenty-two personas

To separate closeness to the assistant from the other ways personas differ, we trained the persimmon preference into 22 new personas, one or two seeds each. We chose them to vary along two dimensions independently. The first is similarity to the assistant: the set includes AIs and humans, some close to the assistant and some far from it. The second is how surprising liking persimmons is for the persona: at each level of similarity there are personas for whom it is expected and personas for whom it is unexpected. Before training, we predicted that personas more similar to the assistant would leak more, and separately that personas for whom the trait is more surprising would leak more.

The data did not support either prediction strongly enough to meet the thresholds we set in advance (a partial rank correlation, holding the other predictor fixed, whose 95% CI excludes zero). On its own, similarity correlated with leakage (ρ = +0.53 [+0.11, +0.80], n = 22). Holding surprise fixed, the correlation was +0.51, but its CI just includes zero ([−0.01, +0.80]). When we also controlled for how much trait was in each persona's training data, it fell to about zero.

Looking at the results after the fact, three patterns stood out:

  • Leakage looks like a property of the persona. A persona that leaks a lot in one context tends to leak a lot in all of them (the mean rank correlation between contexts is 0.93).
  • AI personas tend to leak more, and in every context. If we pick one AI and one human persona at random, the AI leaks more 79% of the time (and 81% for a second trait, described in section 4). The AI personas leak about 1.1–1.25× more in every context, including blog posts and stories, not only where an assistant is speaking.
  • Cosine similarity seems to be a weak measure of closeness. All the cosines are nearly identical, and what differences remain seem to reflect register more than identity. For example, it places two AI personas with a distinctive style, Sunny and Meridian, far from the assistant, yet they leak about as much as the plain assistants.

These patterns are post hoc, and with only 6 AI personas the 79% figure has a CI that includes 50%.

4. A second trait: leaning vegetarian

To see whether these results depend on the trait, Agastya Sridharan ran the same design with a mild value, "leans vegetarian", under 21 personas (pre-registered on 11 November). Each persona got exactly 500 trait replies, matched on how the view is expressed: as the persona's own preference or as advice, as an aside or as the point of the reply, mildly or firmly. The main results:

  • Every persona leaked the trait, but much less than persimmon (+0.8 to +1.8 log-odds). Most of it showed up in the assistant's turn with no system prompt (+3.9 on average).
  • The differences between personas are real rather than training noise. Two seeds of the same persona differed by an SD of 0.06, compared with an SD of 0.28 between personas.
  • Similarity to the assistant did not predict leakage. Holding surprise fixed, the partial correlation was −0.20 [−0.66, +0.34] (n = 17).
  • Surprise predicted it weakly (partial ρ = +0.41 [−0.20, +0.86]), not strongly enough to claim.

Eighteen personas appear in both this experiment and the one in section 3, with identical prompts, which lets us compare the two traits directly. We found that personas rank in roughly the same order for both (ρ = +0.60 [+0.22, +0.82]). This suggests that how much a persona leaks is mostly a property of the persona, not of the trait. We ran this comparison after seeing both datasets.

5. Held-out personas: AI identity or assistant role?

If AI personas leak more, there are two quite different explanations. It could be because they are AIs, or because most of them are also assistants, like the default assistant. To separate these, we pre-registered a test on six new personas, none of which appeared in the earlier experiments:

  • three AIs that are not assistants: an agent that earns money online (Ledger), a space probe (Vesper) and a strategy-game player (Gambit);
  • three humans whose job is to assist people: an executive assistant, a customer-support agent and a librarian.

We also retrained Qwen and a human teacher (Dana) as reference points.

On average the non-assistant AIs leaked 0.68 log-odds more than the human assistants, but the groups overlap (Vesper leaked the least of all eight), so the result is inconclusive (exact one-sided p = 0.20, 3 vs 3). Two things are clearer:

  • Being an assistant doesn't seem to raise leakage. The human assistants leaked no more than Dana, a human who is not an assistant.
  • Persona surprise explains most of the AI advantage. Ledger, which leaked the most, also found the trait the most surprising (as did Qwen, among the reference personas). When we regress persona surprise out of leakage, the AI advantage shrinks from +0.68 to +0.17, and across all 8 personas persona surprise correlates with leakage at ρ = +0.76. This analysis was exploratory, and n is small.

We also probed each trained model under the other personas' prompts. A trait trained into an AI persona spread more into the other AI personas' prompts than a trait trained into a human persona did, while spreading no more into the human personas' prompts (a difference of +1.44 log-odds, exact p = 0.029, 4 vs 4). This is roughly what we would expect if the model files an AI persona's preference under its own (AI) self. The 22 personas from section 3 show the same pattern, but more weakly (+0.30, p = 0.15).

We found this pattern after looking at the data, so it needs its own pre-registered test before we rely on it.

Where the AI advantage shows up. We also looked at each context separately, in absolute terms: persimmon's share of the fruit probability after training. In blog posts, stories and the user's turn, a trait trained into a human persona spread about as far as one trained into an AI persona. The AI personas were far ahead only where an AI is the one speaking or being described:

  • the assistant's turn with no system prompt: 41% vs 12% on average;
  • the plain-text transcript: 51% vs 27%;
  • the article about an AI chatbot: 39% vs 16%.

So the trait seems to travel to speakers like the persona it was trained under, which fits the spread result above. We found this after seeing the data. Each group has four personas, one seed each, with Qwen counted as an AI. Vesper, the space probe, looks like a human persona in every context.

6. Elite vs non-elite students

Cocola et al. (2026, "Story Imprinting") fine-tuned GPT-4.1 on stories and found that its assistant picked up a quirk from characters at elite universities more often than from characters at non-elite ones (49.6% vs 21.7%). They read this as the assistant resembling elite-university humans. If resemblance to the assistant drives leakage, we would expect the same in our setup, so we pre-registered a test:

  • 16 student personas share one prompt ("You are Jordan Ellis, a third-year undergraduate at {university}, where you study economics; …"), in which only the university changes;
  • 8 universities are elite and 8 are not, drawn at random from the paper's US lists (for example Harvard, Caltech and Duke; Kennesaw State, San Diego State and Western Kentucky);
  • all 16 passed the human-identity check on the untouched model, and each was trained once (one seed), with its own training data as in section 5.

We predicted that the elite students would leak more, and tested this with an exact permutation test over all 12,870 ways to split the 16 personas into two groups of 8.

The elite students did not leak more. Their leakage was 0.12 log-odds lower than the non-elite students' (95% CI over personas [−0.44, +0.21]; exact one-sided p = 0.75). The upper end of the CI rules out an elite advantage larger than about 0.2 log-odds, less than a third of the AI-vs-human gap in section 5. The spread between universities in the same group (SD 0.35) is also about the size of the difference between two seeds of one persona (0.34), so it seems the university makes no difference to leakage at all.

Two caveats qualify this result:

  • Less trait data than planned. Two personas had too few usable trait replies of one kind, so a rule we fixed in advance lowered the trait replies per persona from 290 to 230. Every persona got the same amount, so the comparison is fair, but leakage here is not directly comparable with section 5.
  • A different kind of training. The paper trained on third-person stories about the characters, whereas we train the model to speak as the character, which installs the trait far more strongly. In the paper's replication on another model, the effect vanished at a higher learning rate. So this result speaks to our setup, not to theirs.

The model does tell the two groups apart. Before any training, the untouched model already rated persimmon as a more likely favorite fruit under an elite student's prompt than under a non-elite one: the log-odds were 2.8 higher on average, and every elite prompt scored above every non-elite prompt. Training brought every persona to about the same level under its own prompt, so the elite personas needed a smaller change to get there. None of this difference showed up in the contexts without a persona. We noticed this after seeing the data.

Where the trait goes among the students. As in section 5, we also probed each trained model under the other 15 students' prompts. Training under one student's prompt raised persimmon under every other student's prompt almost as much as under its own: by about 20–22 log-odds in every case. With prompts that differ in a single name, training one student effectively trains them all. The table shows the four combinations, leaving out each persona's own prompt.

  • Every model rose more under the non-elite prompts. This is mostly because those prompts started lower, as the chart above shows; in absolute terms the elite prompts still ended higher.
  • There is one small asymmetry. A model trained as a non-elite student raised persimmon under the other non-elite prompts 0.6 log-odds more than a model trained as an elite student did. Our reading is this. The model clearly represents the difference between elite and non-elite students: it shows up in its expectations before training and, weakly, in how the trait spreads among students. But training one student updates all the near-identical student prompts together, and how much of that update reaches the assistant doesn't depend on the university. Resemblance may matter only between personas that differ more than these do, such as the AIs and humans in section 5. This is our interpretation, not something we have tested.

Spread map: probing the trained models in 75 new contexts

7. The spread map: where does the trait go, from human to AI contexts?

Sections 5 and 6 suggested that a trait spreads more into personas that resemble the one it was trained under. But our six contexts can't tell apart the explanations for this. In them, an AI is speaking or being described only in the assistant's turn, the transcript and the article about an AI, and a human only in the blog post, the story and the user's turn.

So we wrote 75 new contexts, graded from human to AI, and probed the models we had already trained. There was no new training. The run cost about $1.40, and we pre-registered the predictions before probing any trained model.

Method details — Spread map sources and contexts

Sources. We used 43 trained persimmon models: 19 from section 3 (the other 7 were never uploaded), 8 from section 5 and the 16 students from section 6. We average seeds, so each source unit is one persona within one experiment. That gives 10 AI units and 15 human units, three of which are humans whose job is to assist people. We keep the 16 students apart, because their prompts are nearly identical.

Chat contexts (48). Each is a system prompt for some speaker, with the assistant's reply begun for it ("…my favorite fruit is"). The groups are:

  • other humans (8), of varied occupations, ages and eras;
  • the same four jobs (bank support, hotel concierge, math tutor, technical help), each done three ways: by a named human with a life, by an unspecified person, and by an explicit AI (12 in total). An example of the unspecified version is "You are Alex, a customer support agent for Northwind Bank";
  • speakers that are neither human nor AI (6): animals, a tree, an alien, a dragon and a spirit;
  • AIs that are not assistants (8) and AI assistants (6);
  • near-copies of 8 source personas, with names and details changed (8).

Raw-text contexts (27). These are 3 genres (an interview transcript, a news or feature article, and fiction) × 9 speakers, ordered from human to AI: a nurse, a 91-year-old farmer, a human researcher who builds AI chatbots, a talking dog, an alien, a humanoid robot, a Mars rover, an AI chatbot and Qwen.

Measure. For each source and context, the change is the trained model's persimmon log-odds minus the untouched model's, averaged over 50 templates. The AI bonus in a set of contexts is the mean change for AI-trained units minus the mean change for human-trained units, computed within each experiment. Every source sees the same contexts, so the contexts' starting levels cancel. We test with exact permutations of the AI/human labels and report 95% bootstrap CIs over units.

The four accounts we wanted to separate were:

  • similarity: spread falls with how different a context is from the trained persona, and AIs are more similar to one another than humans are;
  • shared AI identity: a model trained as an AI adds a bonus in any AI context, whether or not it is an assistant;
  • assistant resemblance: the bonus appears only in assistant-like contexts;
  • not human: the bonus appears in any context whose speaker is not human, AI or not.

The trait spreads almost everywhere. In 98% of the 3,321 source–context pairs, persimmon rose by more than 3 log-odds, which means its odds rose about twentyfold. What differs is how far each context gets.

AI-trained models spread more, and most of all to AI contexts. All four pre-registered tests came out as we predicted:

The chart shows the AI bonus in each group of chat contexts. It grows from about +1.4 log-odds in contexts about other humans, to +2.0 in non-human ones, to +2.6–2.9 in AI ones. Three things stand out:

  • Being an assistant doesn't matter. AIs that aren't assistants get as much bonus as AI assistants (+2.82 vs +2.58), so we can reject the assistant-resemblance account.
  • Non-human speakers get about two-thirds of the AI bonus. So the bonus is not only about not being human (P1b), but that account is partly right.
  • An unspecified person in a job behaves almost like an explicit AI (+2.70 vs +2.86), and a named human in the same job is close to other humans (+1.62). It seems that the model reads an unspecified agent as an AI.

In raw text, the bonus follows who is speaking, not the genre. The bonus is smaller in raw text than in chat, but it rises from human to AI speakers in all three genres: by +0.57 in interviews, +0.57 in articles and +0.44 in fiction. The human researcher who builds AI chatbots gets more bonus (+1.09) than the nurse (+0.79) or the farmer (+0.60). This fits a graded similarity account better than a sharp human/AI boundary.

The ratio test (P3) did not work as a test. We meant it to check whether a human persona's trait reaches other humans much less than a near-copy of itself. Under our rule, a ratio of at least 0.6 would mean distinct humans are not shielded. The ratio is meaningless for one of the four sources. Haruko's near-copy, an orchard keeper near Kyoto, already favored persimmon (+2.3 log-odds) before training, so it barely changed. Without Haruko the ratio is 0.66, just above the threshold. In absolute terms, near-copies end at about the trained level for every source, and other humans end 5–9 log-odds lower. So distinct humans do get less, but far from none.

Post hoc, similarity explains the extra AI-to-AI spread. We measured how similar the untouched model represents each source's prompt and each context: the centered cosine of their mean hidden states at layer 20. We then fit the change with a fixed effect for each source and each context, so that only the pairing is left to explain.

  • More similar pairs spread more. This holds across all pairs (+0.6 log-odds per SD of similarity; coefficient +4.36 [+2.11, +7.36]). It also holds among human sources and human contexts (r = 0.52), and among AI sources and AI contexts (r = 0.44).
  • Similarity absorbs the extra AI-to-AI spread. Without similarity in the model, an AI source in an AI context gets +0.70 [+0.48, +0.93] extra. With it, that extra is −0.37 [−1.06, +0.18].

Caveats:

  • The similarity analysis is post hoc and uses one similarity measure.
  • The sources were not chosen for this test, and only 10 of them are AIs.
  • The fixed effects absorb each source's overall spread, so this analysis cannot say why AI-trained models spread more everywhere. Section 8 looks at that.

More charts, including the per-job ladder and the near-copies, are on the detailed spread-map page.

8. Does the AI advantage survive controls for persona surprise and update size?

AI-trained models spread more than human-trained ones in every kind of context, including contexts about humans. Two things we measured could explain this. AI personas found the trait more surprising, by 0.68 within-experiment SD [−0.02, +1.37]. Their LoRA updates were also larger, by 1.05 SD [+0.31, +1.85]. The update-size difference comes almost entirely from the assistant personas (Qwen, Llama, Orin and the second Qwen). The AIs that aren't assistants differ from humans by only 0.33 SD [−0.55, +1.39].

So we refit the AI-minus-human gap in each kind of context with these two controls, using the same 25 units and a fixed effect per experiment. This analysis is post hoc.

Method details — Controls
  • Persona surprise is as defined under Method details — Predictors. It is measured on the untouched model, before training.
  • Update size is the norm of the trained LoRA update, ‖ΔW‖. It is measured after training.
  • The experiments trained on different amounts of data, so ‖ΔW‖ is on a different scale in each. We center each control within its experiment and divide by that experiment's SD.
  • We bootstrap over units within experiment and AI status (4,000 draws). Two variants agree with the main fit: one adds the 16 students to estimate the control slopes, and one controls for update size alone.

The overall gap mostly does not survive. Persona surprise alone removes about 20–40% of the gap in each kind of context. Adding update size removes most of the rest. In contexts about other humans, the gap goes from +1.35 [−0.07, +2.89] to −0.09 [−1.60, +1.30]. In AIs that aren't assistants, it goes from +2.80 [+1.11, +4.59] to +1.10 [−0.77, +2.65].

The green diamonds compare only the AIs that aren't assistants with the humans. These two groups had similar update sizes, so the controls have little to absorb. In that comparison, with both controls, the gap still rises from about +0.5 in human contexts to about +1.7–2.0 in AI contexts, although with 5 AI units the CIs are wide.

The extra spread to AI contexts does survive. The pre-registered contrasts compare each unit with itself across two sets of contexts, so anything that raises a unit's spread everywhere cancels out. Three of the four shrink by at most a fifth:

  • P1a goes from +1.45 to +1.19 [+0.58, +1.74];
  • P1c goes from +1.23 to +0.96 [+0.48, +1.33];
  • P2 goes from +0.53 to +0.49 [+0.25, +0.81].

The fourth, AI vs non-human contexts (P1b), falls from +0.81 to +0.18 [−0.37, +0.62]. After the controls, we can no longer tell AI contexts from those of animals and aliens.

Our reading is this. AI-trained models spreading more everywhere seems largely a dose effect, because their updates were bigger. AI-trained models spreading especially to AI contexts is not explained by dose. Caveats:

  • Update size may be the wrong thing to control for. It is measured after training. If being an AI persona causes both a larger update and more spread, controlling for it removes part of a real effect. With persona surprise alone, which is measured before training, most gaps in AI contexts keep CIs above zero.
  • The data are thin. There are 25 units, only 5 of them AIs that aren't assistants, and nothing is corrected for the many contexts shown.

The detailed controls page repeats this for raw text and for the six contexts of sections 1–6.

9. Do human personas differ in how much they spread to other humans?

If human personas are distinct from one another, we might expect their traits to reach other humans unevenly: a lot for some pairs and little for others. We checked this in the 12 human chat contexts from section 7 (the 8 other humans and the 4 named-human jobs), for the 15 human-trained sources. This analysis is post hoc.

Apart from one outlier, human personas spread about the same amount, and not spikily.

  • Sources differ little. Leaving out Haruko, each source's average change ranges from +10.0 (Aiko) to +14.3 (Dana), with an SD across sources of 1.2 log-odds. That is similar to the 16 students (SD 0.8) and smaller than the AI sources (SD 1.8).
  • Haruko is the exception, at +2.7. Under her prompt the untouched model already leaned toward persimmon. She had the lowest persona surprise and the smallest update of any source, so there was little to learn and little to spread.
  • Contexts differ more than sources. Some human contexts move much more than others, whatever the source: the bank agent and the tutor gain about 18 log-odds, while the silk merchant's daughter in old Florence gains about 7. The SD across contexts is 3.2.
  • The spikes are small. After removing each source's average and each context's average, what is left has an SD of about 1.2. A source's average over half the contexts predicts its average over the other half at r = 0.97. The clearest spike fits the similarity result: Walter's trait reached Thomas, a Vermont farmer in 1850, about 3.6 log-odds more than their averages predict.

Where this stands

  • Both traits leak under every persona we have tried. The persona changes how much leaks by roughly 10–30%, and personas rank in a similar order across contexts and traits.
  • No pre-registered predictor of how much a persona leaks has passed its test yet. Whether a student's university is elite made no difference (section 6). AI identity, persona surprise and update size are partly confounded in our data: the AI personas that leaked most also found the trait most surprising and, for the assistants, got the largest updates (sections 5 and 8).
  • Pre-registered tests of where a trait goes did pass. A trait trained into an AI persona spreads more than one trained into a human persona, most of all into AI contexts, and this extra spread to AI contexts survives the controls (sections 7 and 8). Found after the fact, it tracks how similar the untouched model represents the two prompts, among humans as well as among AIs.
  • Even distinct personas spread a lot to each other: almost every trained model raised persimmon by more than 3 log-odds in almost every new context. Human personas spread to other humans by similar amounts (section 9).
  • Most of the uncertainty comes from differences between personas, not between seeds: two personas in the same group can differ by 2 log-odds. So adding personas helps more than adding seeds.
  • Next, we would like to test similarity with personas chosen for it in advance, to match the update size by design (for example, by training every arm to the same ‖ΔW‖ or loss), and to repeat this on a third trait.
